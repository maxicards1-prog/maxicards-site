What is a good reaction time?

Lab measurements of reacting to something you see average about 200 milliseconds, and online results are usually a bit higher.

Page updated November 26, 2026.

What measurements say

According to Wikipedia's summary of mental chronometry, which we read on Nov 26, 2026, simple visual reaction time is around 190 milliseconds in young adults, so roughly 200 ms. It changes with the stimulus (a sound is a little faster), the person, tiredness and the way it is measured. We do not give a number labeled as "good," because there is no standard for that.

Start with a quick test

Open the reaction time test and click when the color changes. Do not guess the timing: clicking early cancels the attempt.

Why your result is higher online

In a lab, equipment is tuned for measurement. At home, the path is longer: the screen shows the color with a small delay, the mouse sends the click with another, and the browser and computer add a bit more. Measuring slightly over 200 ms is therefore not unusual.

Getting a fairer measurement

  1. Do five attempts in a row and look at the median, which a single bad try affects less.
  2. Use the same hardware each time. A high refresh rate screen and a wired mouse reduce the device's share of the delay. If you are curious about yours, try checking if your monitor runs at 144Hz.
  3. Stay focused, since a distraction of a few tenths of a second ruins an attempt.
  4. Do not anticipate. Guessing the moment gives misleading numbers.

What can help

  • Being rested. Tiredness slows reactions.
  • Being ready, because sustained attention shortens the time.
  • Practicing a specific task, such as a game, improves your reaction in that task, though not necessarily elsewhere.

Hardware that affects the number

The screen, the mouse, the browser and even the wireless connection each add a small, fixed delay. Two people with different setups cannot fairly compare scores, and you cannot fairly compare the same person on a laptop trackpad and on a gaming mouse. For this reason, treat the result as a personal reference rather than a ranking.

What not to conclude

An online score is not a medical diagnosis or an aptitude test. If you are worried about an unusual slowdown, talk to a health professional.

A different skill: clicking fast

The CPS test measures something else: how quickly your finger repeats clicks, not how fast you react to a signal. See what is a good CPS for how to read that.

If you want to compare

Keep a log: date, hardware, median of five attempts. Comparing your own sessions is more informative than any average, because hardware and method differ so much between people.

Frequently asked questions

Is my reaction time the same for sounds?

A sound usually gives a slightly shorter time than something you see. Our test uses a visual signal.

Is this a medical test?

No. It is for curiosity and for comparing your own attempts, and it cannot diagnose anything.

Why is my online result slower than 200 ms?

Screen delay, mouse delay and browser overhead all add a little time that a lab setup avoids.
