How to Tell If a Pokémon Card Is Fake

In short No single test is enough, but six checks done in order will catch most fakes. Here is the method, its limits, and the habits to have before you buy.

Updated November 24, 2026 · Authenticity and condition · by the MaxiCards team

Check a Pokémon card in 6 steps

  1. Compare with a real card. Place the card next to a genuine card from the same era, ideally the same set. Compare size, thickness, stiffness and how vivid the colors are.
  2. Look at the back. The back of a real card is a deep blue, with a sharp logo and a well-centered Poké Ball, red side up. Dull colors, a blurry logo or an off-center Poké Ball are bad signs.
  3. Read all the text. Look for the accent on “Pokémon”, the fonts, the size of the energy symbols, the colors of the name and HP, and any sentences that do not match the Pokémon shown.
  4. Check borders and artwork. An unusually thick border, artwork that does not match the official card, or a gold border on a card that should not have one are all warning signs.
  5. Do the light test. Put a flashlight behind the card. A real card lets very little light through; a card that lights up evenly is suspect. It is a clue, not a proof.
  6. Feel it and tilt it. Feel the texture, flex it very slightly without creasing it, and tilt the card to judge the holo. A card that is too soft, or a missing texture, adds to the doubt.

Why one test is never enough

Counterfeiters copy what you can see first: the artwork, the colors, the name. They more often get wrong what you have to measure or feel: the thickness of the card stock, the finish of the holo, the precision of the small print. That is why the method above relies on several clues that support each other. A single flaw can be a real printing error. Three or four flaws together are a very worrying sign.

Keep in mind that a card that passes every test can still be fake. According to the collector guides we read, some counterfeits can only be told apart from the original under heavy magnification. For a valuable card, comparing it with a card you know is real, or getting a professional opinion, is the only real safety net.

What each clue tells you

Text and symbols

Cardmarket's help page on counterfeits lists a missing accent on the “é” in “Pokémon” first: an error like that rarely happens by accident in real printing. The same page also points to energy symbols that are too big or off-center in their circle, unusual font colors (a green name or red HP, for example), leftover text from another card (a description that talks about a different Pokémon), and an inconsistent layout, such as a Basic Pokémon showing an evolution.

Colors and card stock

A real card looks vivid. Fakes often look washed out or, the other way round, oversaturated, and their surface is often glossier, with a varnish-like shine. As for size, a standard card measures about 63 mm by 88 mm (roughly 2.5 by 3.5 inches). A slightly different size, or a card that feels clearly softer or thinner than the others, is suspicious.

Gold borders

A gold border only exists on a few specific cards. Cardmarket mentions the Delta Species holo rares and the gold secret rares. A gold border anywhere else should raise an eyebrow. Our guide on gold cards covers this case in detail: gold Pokémon cards: real or fake.

Holo pattern and texture

On cards with a textured finish (GX cards, for example), the texture is rarely copied well. Some real but badly printed cards do not have the full texture either, so use this check together with the others. In the same way, a holo pattern that does not match a reference card (circles instead of stars, say) is a good warning sign.

The light test: what is it really worth?

Many guides explain that a real card is opaque because its stock has a dark layer between two white layers, while a counterfeit often lets light through more evenly. It is a useful clue, but let us be clear: Cardmarket's help page on counterfeits does not describe this test, so it is not an official proof. Use it as one clue among others, with your phone's flashlight behind the card, and always compare with a card you are sure about.

Never tear a card apart to look inside the stock. That test is destructive, and it can only be justified on a worthless card whose origin is already obvious.

Impossible details

Some mistakes are conclusive because they describe something that does not exist. There are only “1st Edition” and “Unlimited” prints, so a “2nd Edition” card is necessarily fake, as Cardmarket points out. For sealed boosters and boxes, the same page flags the Nintendo name on a recent product, the words “Pokémon USA Inc.” on a product made after 2009, an illustration of Ash on a European or English-language product, a copyright date that does not match the product, or several weld points on the side of the plastic wrap. For genuine first editions, see our guide on Pokémon 1st Edition cards.

Before you buy: three habits

  • A price far below the usual market price is the first sign of a scam. If you cannot explain the difference, walk away.
  • Ask for sharp photos of the front, the back and the edge, taken in daylight, with no filter.
  • Prefer sellers and platforms that protect the buyer, and keep every trace of the exchange. Our guide on how to avoid Pokémon card scams covers the traps of re-weighed boosters and fake listings.

If you doubt an important card

If the card matters to you, do not rely on your eye alone. Grading companies authenticate cards before sealing them in a slab: read what PSA grading is and what it does to understand what you actually get. To estimate what a genuine card is worth, use the method in how to estimate the value of a Pokémon card. Frequently asked questions

Is the light test enough to know if a Pokémon card is real?

No. It is a useful clue, but Cardmarket does not describe it in its counterfeit guide, and a card can pass it and still be fake. Combine it with the comparison, the back and the text.

Can you check a Pokémon card with an app?

Apps exist, but we found no independent source proving they are reliable. Treat their verdict as a clue, never as a certification.

What should I do if I bought a fake Pokémon card?

Keep the listing, the messages and the photos, then contact the seller and open a dispute on the platform you paid through. Do not resell it as genuine.

Is a Pokémon card with no accent on the é always fake?

According to Cardmarket it is a very strong warning sign, since the error is unlikely in real printing. Still look for other clues before you conclude.
