How to Find Out What a Pokémon Card Is Worth

In short There is no single price for a card: it depends on the exact version, the language, the condition and recent sales. Here is a simple method to get an honest price range without trusting an outdated price list.

Updated November 24, 2026 · Value, buying and selling · by the MaxiCards team

How to value a Pokémon card in 6 steps

  1. Identify the card. Note the name, the number (for example 123/191) and the set symbol at the bottom of the card, plus the language.
  2. Check it is real. Make sure the card is genuine before looking up any price: a copy cannot be sold.
  3. Judge the condition. Look at corners, edges, surface and back under good light, then pick the condition cautiously, without rounding up.
  4. Find real sales. On Cardmarket and in eBay's Sold items, note several sales of the same version, in the same language and a similar condition.
  5. Work out the net. Subtract fees, packaging and postage with the fee calculator to see what you will really receive.
  6. Decide. Sell the card on its own, in a lot, or keep it. For a very expensive card in perfect shape, look into grading.

A Pokémon card has no “official price”. Its value is what buyers are actually willing to pay today for exactly the same card. That number moves: a card can double after a game announcement, an anniversary or a much-hyped release, then fall back a few months later. This is why this guide gives no price list. It shows you how to find the price yourself, every time, in a few minutes.

Asking price vs. sold price: the difference that matters

The first mistake is looking at current listings. A seller can ask for anything, and nothing proves anyone will pay it. Only completed sales count. On eBay, use the “Sold items” filter in the search. On Cardmarket, look at the price history and trend price on the card page, as well as the cheapest offers for your language and condition. One lone price proves nothing: look for several sales close together.

The four things that change the price

  • The exact version. The same artwork often exists in several rarities (regular, reverse holo, alternate art, gold…) and in several sets. The number printed at the bottom of the card tells them apart: see how to read a Pokémon card and the rarity symbols.
  • The language. Japanese, English and other-language cards from the same set do not have the same value. Always compare like for like (Japanese or English cards?).
  • The condition. A whitened corner or a scratch on the holo surface lowers the price, sometimes a lot. Cardmarket advises sellers to be strict: if in doubt, grade the card one level lower. The details are in the guide to Mint and Near Mint conditions.
  • The authenticity. A fake card is worth nothing. Before valuing anything, read how to tell if a Pokémon card is fake.

Useful price sources

None is enough on its own. Cross-check at least two:

  • Cardmarket for single cards in Europe: many sellers, standardized conditions, prices by language.
  • eBay “Sold items”: handy for rare, graded or older cards that sell at auction.
  • Auction house results for exceptional cards: they are public and dated, but they concern top-graded cards, rarely the contents of an ordinary binder. A record like those in the most expensive Pokémon card in the world says nothing about the price of your card.

Be wary of “price guide” sites that show no date and no source: they sometimes copy old prices. Be wary too of scanning apps that promise an instant price: they regularly get the version or the language wrong. Use them to find the name of a card, not to set a price.

From market price to what you will actually receive

The sale price is not what lands in your account. You have to subtract the platform fee, the packaging and the postage. On Cardmarket, the official fee table lists 5% of the item value, capped at €100 per item and rounded up to the next cent. eBay and other marketplaces have their own fee schedules, which change often: check the current page for your country before you list. The Cardmarket and eBay fee calculator does the maths for you.

When to think about grading

Having a card graded by a company such as PSA can raise its resale price, but it costs money, takes time and the result is never guaranteed. Keep it for cards whose raw price already justifies the expense and whose condition looks flawless. See what PSA is and how to get a card graded.

What to keep in mind

  • A rare card is not always expensive, and a common card from a popular set can be: demand matters as much as rarity.
  • A range is more honest than a single number: “between this and that, depending on condition”.
  • Run your search again on the day you sell: last week’s prices may not be today’s.
  • Cards are not a safe investment: this guide helps you estimate, not invest.

Have a whole binder to go through? Start with the quick two-minute sort, then value only the cards that stand out.

Frequently asked questions

Is there a reliable app to find out what a card is worth?

Apps help you identify a card, but their prices are often rough or out of date. Always check the result on Cardmarket or in eBay's sold listings.

Is a holographic card always worth more?

No. Shine does not set the price: the rarity of the version, the character, the set and the condition do. Many holo cards remain very cheap.

Why does the price change from one week to the next?

Because demand follows the news (new games, anniversaries, collector videos) and because the number of copies for sale changes. Only recent sales give a fair idea.

Should I get a card graded before selling it?

Not automatically. Grading has a cost and a waiting time; it mostly makes sense for a card that is already expensive and in excellent condition.
