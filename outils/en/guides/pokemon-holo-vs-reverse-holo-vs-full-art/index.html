Pokémon Holo, Reverse Holo, Full Art and Alternate Art: What Is the Difference?

In short Four terms come up constantly in listings and booster pulls. They describe a card's finish or layout, not its price. Here is how to recognize each one, with the nuances worth knowing.

Updated November 24, 2026 · Reading a card · by the MaxiCards team

Finish and layout: two different things

To make sense of it, separate two ideas. The finish describes where and how the card shines (holo, reverse holo). The layout describes how the artwork fills the card (full art, alternate art). One card can combine both, and neither says how rare the card is: for that, read Pokémon card rarity symbols.

The holo (holographic) card

A holo card shines in the artwork area: the drawing of the Pokémon reflects light and changes look as you tilt the card. According to Pokepedia, the Rare rarity (black star) can be holographic in the artwork, and a card with the holo star symbol is always holographic. Promo cards have no rarity symbol but can still be holographic. Before trusting a card's shine, remember that holo is also one of the most imitated features in counterfeits: see how to tell if a Pokémon card is fake.

The reverse holo card

Pokepedia describes the reverse holo as a holographic version distinct from the purely holo card: it is generally shiny across the whole surface, sometimes except for the artwork. In other words, the shine is on the background and borders, whereas on a regular holo card it is in the picture. A reverse holo can be made from a card of any rarity in the set, and in some sets only certain cards exist as reverse holos.

Practical consequence: a reverse holo Common or Uncommon is still a Common or Uncommon. Its price depends on demand for that Pokémon, its condition and the set, not on the finish alone. Note that, according to Cardotaku, classic Japanese sets have no reverse holo cards; every rare in them is holographic. For the comparison between markets, see the Japanese-versus-other-languages pages on fan wikis.

The full art card

Pokepedia defines a Full Art card as one where the artwork covers the whole surface, with no border, and the effects and attacks are printed over the image. The format first appeared with the Black & White set and has existed in every set since. In Japan, these cards are secret rares numbered beyond the set total; in the West, they are part of the numbering, usually near the end. Some Trainer cards also get the Full Art treatment, often two per set.

Pokepedia's page does not cover GX, V or VMAX Full Arts: for those cards, compare with set lists and official artwork rather than trusting a single criterion.

The alternate art card

“Alternate art” or “alt art” refers to cards whose artwork is done in a style different from the regular versions of the same Pokémon, often with a full scene. According to Bleeding Cool, this type appeared in the Sun & Moon era with Team Up, after an isolated case of Mewtwo-GX in Shining Legends. It came back in force in Sword & Shield with Battle Styles, according to InvenGlobal, with six highly sought-after cards. Since Scarlet & Violet, the Illustration Rare (gold star) and Special Illustration Rare (two gold stars) rarities carry on that idea of a picture that tells a scene.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a holo and a reverse holo Pokémon card?

On a holo, the shine is in the artwork; on a reverse holo, it is on the rest of the card, sometimes except for the picture.

Is a reverse holo rarer than a normal card?

Not necessarily. A reverse holo can come from any rarity in the set, including Commons and Uncommons.

When did the full art format start?

Pokepedia says it first appeared with the Black & White set and has existed in every set since.

What is an alternate art card?

A card whose artwork is drawn in a different style from the regular version of the same Pokémon, often with a full background. The type first appeared during the Sun & Moon series.
