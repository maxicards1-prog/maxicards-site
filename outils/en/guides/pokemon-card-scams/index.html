How to Avoid Pokémon Card Scams

In short Soaring prices have attracted scammers: fake booster packs, resealed products, deals that look too good. Here are the most common traps, the warning signs and the protections platforms really offer.

Updated November 24, 2026 · Value, buying and selling · by the MaxiCards team

The more sought after a product is, the more it attracts fraudsters, and Pokémon cards are no exception. The most common scams involve sealed products (booster packs, boxes, displays), valuable cards and payments. No advice replaces caution, but a few habits avoid most bad surprises. This guide accuses no particular seller: it describes types of traps.

Trap 1: fake or resealed booster packs

A resealed pack is an opened pack, emptied of its best cards, then closed again. A fake pack is a complete copy. According to a specialist retailer, here are the signs to check on the wrapper:

  • an uneven, jagged or shifted heat seal (it should be straight and smooth);
  • a doubled seal, visible under raking light: the trace of a reopening;
  • plastic that is too thin, too stiff or odd to the touch;
  • dull or over-saturated colors, an official watermark that is missing or blurry;
  • altered legal text on the back;
  • a price well below the market.

In a sealed display (booster box), the packs are stacked the same way: mixed-up orientation is suspect, because it can indicate a rebuilt box. Before buying a sealed product, ask for sharp photos of the seal and for its provenance. To see what a box officially contains, read booster box, ETB, collection box: which to choose.

Trap 2: refurbished boxes and Elite Trainer Boxes

Boxes can be opened, stripped of the most sought-after packs, then closed again with film. Again, a price below retailers’ and no photos of the original packaging should raise alarms. Consumer reports of resealed packs, even in stores, show that this risk exists at resellers too: keep your proof of purchase and, for a big purchase, film yourself opening the product.

Trap 3: fake cards

Counterfeits circulate, sometimes in bulk: according to the French consumer magazine Que Choisir, 7.6 tonnes of counterfeit cards bound for Europe were seized in Shanghai in late November 2021. The clues (paper, printing, holo, back) are detailed in how to tell if a card is fake. An expensive card without a sharp photo, or sold far below market, is suspect. Be wary of vague wording like “rare edition”: a specialist retailer points out that Pokémon never published a “2nd edition”.

Trap 4: payment outside the platform

The classic request: “I’m outside the platform, let’s pay by bank transfer or send money to friends and family.” These methods give you no recourse. Whether buying or selling, stay inside the platform’s own payment flow:

  • Cardmarket: the Trustee Service holds the payment until receipt is confirmed for tracked shipments, and registered shipping is required above €25.
  • eBay: its money back guarantee covers items not received or not as described, but you must report the problem within the time limit set in the current rules. The buyer protection fees paid by buyers cover customer service, fraud detection and secure payments, according to eBay.
  • Second-hand apps: buyers usually pay a protection fee that covers the transaction made on the platform. Check the current rules in your country.

Trap 5: fake listings and fake websites

  • A “too good to be true” price on a site you do not know.
  • A site with no legal notice, no address, or only glowing reviews.
  • Pressure to pay fast (“last one”, “10 minutes left”).
  • Photos taken from the internet, identical to those in other listings.

Before ordering, search the seller’s or site’s name with the word “reviews”, look for its legal details and check that it offers secure payment. If you suspect fraud, report it to your national consumer protection authority.

Checklist before buying

  • Does the seller have a history and reviews going back several months?
  • Is the price in line with other trusted sellers? For a sealed product, compare with the price-per-booster calculator.
  • Do the photos show the seal, the angles and the back?
  • Does payment go through the platform?
  • Have you kept the messages, photos and proof of payment?

When in doubt, walk away. A missed bargain costs less than a scam.

Frequently asked questions

How can I tell if a Pokémon booster pack has been resealed?

Look at the heat seal under raking light: a doubled, shifted or jagged seal is suspect, as are odd plastic, a blurry watermark or a very low price.

Can an Elite Trainer Box bought in a store be refurbished?

Cases of resealed packs have been reported, even at resellers. Keep proof of purchase and, for a big purchase, film the opening.

What should I do if I was scammed on a platform?

Contact the seller, then open a dispute within the platform's time limit. Keep all messages and report the seller or site to your consumer protection authority.

Is it safe to pay a private seller by bank transfer?

No: a bank transfer gives you no simple recourse. Always prefer paying through the platform, which protects the buyer.
