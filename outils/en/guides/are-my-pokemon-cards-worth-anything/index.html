How to Tell If Your Pokémon Cards Are Worth Anything

In short Faced with a binder or a box of cards, you quickly wonder which ones deserve a real look. This guide gives a fast three-pile sort to do before you look up any prices.

Updated November 24, 2026 · Value, buying and selling · by the MaxiCards team

Found Pokémon cards in an attic, a drawer or a grown-up kid’s binder? Before you throw them out or sell them by the pound, a quick sort picks out the cards worth a closer look. The goal is not to find the exact price but to spot the candidates. The precise price comes next, using the method in how to value a Pokémon card.

A starting principle: most cards are common

According to the official page for a Mega Evolution display, each booster pack holds 10 cards and a basic Energy card. In a pack, most cards are Commons and Uncommons, printed in huge numbers. They are worth very little, sometimes a few cents. The value is concentrated in a minority of cards: certain rarities, well-kept older cards, promos and a few cards of very popular characters. There is nothing to worry about here: that is how a collectible game works.

The three-pile sort, with no cleaning

Spread your cards on a clean table, wash your hands, and make three piles.

Pile 1: look closer

  • Older cards. The very first sets (late 1990s and early 2000s) are the most sought after when in good condition. Look for a “1st Edition” stamp (see the guide) or the mention of an old set.
  • Cards with a high rarity symbol. Star, double star, gold star, special symbol. The guide to rarity symbols explains them.
  • Full art, alternate art or gold cards. Artwork that spills across the whole card, a textured background, a gold finish. Also read holo, reverse holo and full art and gold Pokémon cards.
  • Promos and event cards. They usually carry a “PROMO” mention or an event logo. Look near the card number or the set symbol.
  • Japanese cards and other languages. Some sets are in high demand (details here).
  • Popular characters (Charizard, Pikachu, Eevee, Mew…), especially in rare versions. It is not enough on its own, but it adds to the interest.

Pile 2: ordinary cards

Commons, Uncommons, basic Energy, regular versions of everyday cards. Keep them if you like them, or sell them as a lot. They do not justify looking up one by one.

Pile 3: set aside or check

Very worn, creased, wet or pen-marked cards: their value is minimal, even for a rare card. Cards with an odd feel, a back that is too light or blurry printing: these may be copies. Run them through the authenticity check before getting your hopes up.

Do not clean, rub or press

A common reflex is to wipe a dusty card. Don’t: a cloth damages the surface, water warps the card and a cleaning product can lift the ink. If a card is merely dusty, blow on it or use a dry microfiber cloth without pressing. Then store your best cards in a sleeve (protecting your cards). Never peel off a label or sticker residue: you could pull off the ink.

False leads

  • “It’s old, so it’s worth a lot.” Not necessarily: some old sets were printed in enormous quantities. Condition and rarity count far more than age alone.
  • “It’s shiny, so it’s rare.” The holo effect exists on very common cards.
  • “I saw the same one for a huge price.” An asking price is not a paid price. One example of volatility: according to the French site Journal du Geek, a PSA 10 Raincoat Pikachu sold for $19,200 in 2025, then another copy fetched several million dollars in autumn 2026. One copy’s record says nothing about the value of another card with the same name.

What next?

For each card in pile 1, note the name, number, language and condition, then look up completed sales of the same version. If several cards have value, compare platforms in where to sell your Pokémon cards. The rest can go as a lot, without spending hours chasing a few cents. To see whether a sale will pay off after fees, use the fee calculator.

Finally, remember one simple rule: until you have checked real sales, believe neither a passing “expert” nor an ad promising fortunes in old binders.

Frequently asked questions

Are my childhood Pokémon cards worth anything?

Sometimes, but rarely a lot. Older cards in excellent condition, promos and certain rarities can have value; played, creased or common cards are worth very little.

Does a bent or damaged card keep any value?

Yes, but much less than a copy in good condition. A damaged rare card can still sell, at a far lower price.

Should I sell a whole binder in one go?

It saves time, but you risk selling a good card at the price of a common. Sort first, then sell the good cards one by one and the rest as a lot.

How do I recognize a promo card?

It usually carries a PROMO mention or a number different from the cards in a regular set. The guide on how to read a card explains these markers.
