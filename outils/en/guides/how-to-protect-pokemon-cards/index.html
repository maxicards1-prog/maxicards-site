How to protect your Pokémon cards, from commons to chase cards

In short Good protection costs very little compared with a damaged card. Here is what to use depending on a card's value, and how to store it.

Updated November 24, 2026 · Protection and storage · by the MaxiCards team

How to protect a Pokémon card in 6 steps

  1. Wash and dry your hands. Oily or damp skin leaves marks on the glossy surface. Hold the card by the edges and do not touch the center.
  2. Slide the card into a penny sleeve. Insert it gently, corner first. If it catches, check that the sleeve is fully open.
  3. Add a rigid holder for valuable cards. Put the sleeved card in a 3 x 4 inch toploader or a semi-rigid holder, and do not close it with tape.
  4. Choose where to store it. PVC-free binder pages for collections, a box for bulk, a small rigid box for valuable cards.
  5. Choose the right spot. A room with a stable temperature, away from sun and damp. Avoid attics, basements and radiators.
  6. Check in from time to time. Once a year, open the binder or box and look for damp, warping, mold or sleeves sticking together. Fix problems right away.

The threats to know about

A cardboard card mostly gets damaged in four ways: knocks and rubbing (crushed corners, whitened edges), bends and creases, humidity and light. Paper conservators recommend keeping relative humidity between 30% and 60% and limiting light exposure, because light damage is cumulative and cannot be undone; UV light yellows and weakens paper. Big swings in temperature and humidity should be avoided too, which in practice rules out attics, basements and poorly insulated rooms.

Choose by the card's value

  • Commons and cheap cards: a binder with pockets is enough, or even a box, with the cards stored upright.
  • Rare or played cards: a sleeve, and optionally a second, larger sleeve around the first (double sleeving) to limit edge wear.
  • Valuable cards: a sleeve plus a toploader or semi-rigid holder, stored upright in a box rather than stacked flat under weight.
  • Very expensive cards: grading and a sealed slab, once you have worked out the cost with our fee calculator.

Watch the material

Avoid sleeves and pages made of PVC (sometimes called vinyl). Conservation guides for photographs and documents consider it chemically unstable, and over time it can damage what it holds. Uncoated polypropylene and polyester enclosures are considered safer. Look for "PVC-free" on the packaging. Binders get their own treatment in our guide on choosing a card binder.

Everyday habits

  • Handle cards with clean, dry hands and hold them by the edges.
  • Slide the card into the sleeve gently, without forcing it, so you do not crush a corner.
  • Never put a rubber band directly on a card.
  • Do not use tape on a card, or on a sleeve you want to keep looking new.
  • Keep cards away from windows, direct sun, bathrooms and kitchens.
  • Store them upright in a box or binder so they do not warp.

Graded cards and cards you are shipping

A grading company's slab is both protection and proof of condition: do not open it. If you are sending a card to be graded, do not put it in just any holder: PSA, for example, asks for a sleeve and then a semi-rigid holder, and does not accept toploaders for submissions. The detailed rules are in our guide on how to get a card graded.

A protected card that is badly stored is still vulnerable. To get organized, read how to organize and sort your Pokémon cards.

Frequently asked questions

Should you sleeve all your Pokémon cards?

It is not required, but it is cheap. At least sleeve your rare cards, valuable cards and the ones you play with, and keep the rest in a binder.

Does double sleeving damage cards?

If the sleeves are too tight, sliding a card in can rub the corners. Choose an inner sleeve that does not squeeze the card and an outer one that is slightly larger.

What is the best holder for a valuable card?

It depends on the use: a toploader for storage, a semi-rigid holder for sending to a grader, a sealed slab for a card that is already graded. None of them replaces dry, stable storage away from light.

Can you clean a dirty Pokémon card?

Avoid water or cleaning products: they can damage the surface, and a card that has been altered can be refused for grading. A dry microfiber cloth, without rubbing, is the safe limit.
