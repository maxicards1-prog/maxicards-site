ChatGPT Prompts for Teachers

Ten structured prompts to save time on planning, exercises and communication, without giving up your own professional judgment.

  2. Create graded exercises

  7. Write report card comments
  8. Write a note to families
  9. Brainstorm a cross-curricular project

Planning a lesson, writing ten versions of an exercise, rewording instructions: these tasks take time and are a good fit for AI help. The prompts below give the AI a role, a context, constraints and a response format.

1. Plan a unit of lessons

To structure a series of lessons around a concept in the curriculum.

You are an experienced [subject] teacher at [grade level]. I am preparing a unit of [number] lessons on the following concept: [concept].
Context: a class of [number of students] students, [special features: mixed ability, students who struggle with reading, etc.], lessons of [length].
Propose a unit plan: the goal of each lesson, the main activity, the expected notes or written output, and an end-of-unit assessment.
Present the result as a table. Before answering, ask me any questions you need.

2. Create graded exercises

When you want exercises from easiest to hardest on the same concept.

You are a [subject] teacher. Create [number] exercises on [concept] for [grade level] students.
Constraints: three difficulty levels (introduction, practice, extension), short problem statements, age-appropriate vocabulary, varied real-life contexts.
Format: for each exercise, the problem, then the detailed answer in a separate section at the end.
Point out the places where you might make a mistake, so I can check them.

3. Differentiate an activity

To adapt the same task for students at different levels.

Here is an activity I give my [grade level] [subject] class:
[paste the instructions]
Rewrite it in three versions: a simplified version (instructions broken into steps, lighter vocabulary, support provided), a standard version, and an extension version (a more open-ended question).
All three versions must aim at the same learning objective: [objective].
Keep a supportive tone and short sentences.

4. Build a grading rubric

To assess a piece of work (presentation, essay, project) with clear criteria.

You are an instructional coach. Help me build a grading rubric for [type of work: presentation, essay, project] in [subject], [grade level].
Target skills: [list].
Expected format: a table with 4 to 6 criteria, 4 performance levels described with observable statements (no vague judgments), and weighting out of [total] points.
Finish with a student-friendly version of the rubric, in simple language, so they understand what is expected.

5. Write a review quiz

To quickly create an end-of-chapter quiz.

You are a [subject] teacher at [grade level]. Using the chapter summary below, create a quiz of [number] questions: multiple choice with 4 options (only one correct answer), short-answer questions, and one open-ended question.
Chapter summary: [paste your summary]
Format: number the questions, then give a separate answer key with a short explanation for each answer.
Use only the information in the summary and do not invent any extra facts.

6. Reexplain a difficult concept

When an explanation did not land and you need another approach.

I need to explain the concept "[concept]" to [grade level] students who did not understand my first explanation, which was: [summary of your explanation].
Suggest three different ways to explain it: an everyday analogy, a diagram described in words that I can draw on the board, and a short step-by-step example.
For each one, point out any risk of confusion. Use short sentences and accessible vocabulary.

7. Write report card comments

To vary the style of your comments, based on your own observations.

You are a teacher writing report card comments. Here are my observations about a student, with no name: strengths: [list]; difficulties: [list]; trend: [progress, plateau, decline]; advice: [suggested focus].
Write 3 versions of a comment of [number] words maximum: supportive, factual, encouraging.
Professional tone, no judgment about the person, concrete advice. Do not include any name.

8. Write a note to families

For an announcement, reminder or meeting request, written with tact.

You are a [grade level] teacher. Write a message to families about: [topic: field trip, supply reminder, meeting, behavior concern].
Information to include: [date, place, action required, deadline].
Constraints: polite and clear tone, 120 words maximum, a request for action in bold at the end, no educational jargon.
Offer two versions: a formal version (printed note) and a short version for the school messaging app.

9. Brainstorm a cross-curricular project

To find ideas for a project linking two or three subjects.

You are an instructional coordinator. Help me come up with a cross-curricular project between [subject 1] and [subject 2] for [grade level] students, on the theme [theme].
Constraints: duration of [length], almost no budget, a visible final product (exhibit, short play, poster, newspaper).
For each idea, give: the final product, the role of each subject, the skills involved, and possible difficulties.
Give me 3 ideas, from the simplest to the most ambitious.

10. Simplify a text for easier reading

To adapt a text for students who struggle with reading or are learning English.

Here is a text for [grade level] students: [paste the text]
Rewrite it for students who struggle with reading: sentences of 15 words maximum, everyday vocabulary, difficult words explained in parentheses, key ideas in bold.
Keep the exact meaning: do not remove any essential information. Then add 4 comprehension questions of increasing difficulty, with the answers.

What to check before using it in class

An AI can write a wrong problem with total confidence, especially in math, history or science. Solve every exercise yourself before handing it out, and check dates, formulas and quotations. Always compare the result with your curriculum standards and the expectations for your grade level.

Data you should never paste

Do not enter any personal student data: names, photos, named grades, family situation, health or disability information. Prepare report card comments from anonymous observations. Student privacy laws cover minors' data, and these services sometimes store what you type. Also follow the guidelines of your school and district.

Limits

The AI does not know your students. It replaces neither your experience nor your judgment about pacing. Use it to get a first draft, then personalize it for your class and your style, the way you would with a textbook.

Frequently asked questions

Can I use ChatGPT to grade papers?

You can ask it to help you design a scoring guide, but do not give it named student work. Grading is a matter of your professional judgment. If you test a tool, use anonymized work, review every correction and keep the final decision.

Do these prompts work with Claude and Gemini?

Yes. They rely on a simple structure (role, context, constraints and format) that every assistant understands. Answers vary from one tool to another: compare them and keep the one that suits your class.

How do I avoid answers that are too generic?

Give the grade level, the exact concept, the length of the lesson and your students' difficulties. The more precise the context, the more useful the answer. You can also ask the AI to ask you its questions before it answers.
