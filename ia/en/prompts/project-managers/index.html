ChatGPT Prompts for Project Managers

Ten prompts to scope, plan, track risks and communicate on your projects, without handing off your trade-off decisions.

In short

  1. Write a project brief
  2. Break a project into work packages
  3. Propose a backward schedule
  4. Identify and rank risks
  5. Map the stakeholders
  6. Prepare a status update for the steering committee
  7. Trade off scope, time and cost
  8. Handle a change request
  9. Run a retrospective
  10. Write the project closeout report

A project manager juggles scope, deadlines, budget and people. An AI is useful for structuring a project brief, anticipating risks and formatting documents, not for deciding in your place.

The more you describe your real context (team, constraints, deadlines), the more usable the answers. Replace the brackets, then review with your knowledge of the field.

  • State the method used (waterfall, agile, hybrid).
  • Ask for hypotheses to validate, not certainties.
  • Keep internal names and numbers out of the prompt.

1. Write a project brief

To set goals, scope and success criteria before starting.

You are a senior project manager in [industry]. Help me write the project brief for the following project: [description in 3 sentences].
Context: sponsor [role], team of [number] people, desired deadline [date], indicative budget [order of magnitude].
Expected structure: background and stakes, measurable objectives, in-scope items, out-of-scope items, deliverables, assumptions, constraints, success criteria, initial risks.
First ask me the 6 questions you are missing. Do not invent any number: mark what is missing as "to be confirmed".

2. Break a project into work packages

When the scope is clear but not yet turned into tasks.

Here are the deliverables of a project: [list of deliverables]. Target total duration: [length]. Skills available: [list].
Build a work breakdown structure (WBS) on three levels: work packages, sub-packages, tasks. For each package, give the associated deliverable, the profile needed and an estimate in days as a range.
Flag the tasks whose estimate is very uncertain and suggest a question to ask the team to refine it. Present the result as a table.

3. Propose a backward schedule

To start from a deadline and work back through the milestones.

I must deliver [deliverable] on [date]. Known steps: [list with dependencies]. People unavailable: [periods]. Holidays or closures to take into account: [list].
Propose a backward schedule with milestones, durations, dependencies and buffers. Identify the critical path and tell me which tasks can be done in parallel.
Add 3 possible catch-up scenarios if a milestone slips by a week. Format: table, then a 5-line summary for the sponsor.

4. Identify and rank risks

To feed a risk register with ideas you have not thought of.

You are a project risk management specialist. Project: [description]. Context: [team, technologies or methods, suppliers, tight schedule or not].
List 12 plausible risks, grouped by category (schedule, budget, technical, people, external). For each: cause, consequence, probability and impact on a 1 to 3 scale, preventive action, contingency action, warning sign to watch.
Present a table, then the 3 risks to address first with your reasoning. I will judge for myself what is realistic.

5. Map the stakeholders

To decide who to inform, consult or involve, and how.

Here are the groups affected by my project (roles, not names): [list]. What the project changes for them: [description].
Build a stakeholder map: for each group, their interest, their level of influence, their likely attitude (supportive, neutral, reluctant), what they need to move forward, and a communication strategy (frequency, channel, key message).
Finish with a 30-day action plan for the two most critical groups.

6. Prepare a status update for the steering committee

To present the project's status clearly and honestly.

I am presenting the progress of the project [generic name] to the steering committee in [length] minutes. Situation: actual progress [description], schedule variances [description], budget variances [description], open issues [list], decisions I am waiting for: [list].
Structure a 5-slide deck: summary (project health), progress, variances and causes, risks, decisions requested.
Write the text of each slide in 40 words maximum and suggest 5 questions the committee might ask, with elements of an answer. Do not downplay any variance.

7. Trade off scope, time and cost

When a constraint changes and you need to offer options to the sponsor.

My project [description] is facing the following change: [delay, budget cut, request for an addition]. Non-negotiable constraints: [list]. Possible flexibility: [list].
Propose three realistic trade-off scenarios: reduce the scope, move the date, add resources. For each scenario: consequences, risks, what to ask the sponsor, what I lose.
Present a comparison table and a reasoned recommendation, flagging the assumptions I must verify with the team.

8. Handle a change request

To analyze the impact of a late request from a client or sponsor.

A requester ([role]) wants to add the following to the ongoing project: [description of the request]. Project status: [phase, progress].
Write an impact analysis sheet: description of the request, the real need behind the request, impact on schedule, budget, quality and other deliverables, options (accept, postpone, decline, reduced version), recommendation.
Then write a 100-word reply message to the requester, factual and courteous, that proposes the next step.

9. Run a retrospective

To draw concrete lessons from a phase or a finished project.

I need to run a [length] retrospective with [number] people after [phase or project]. What happened: [facts, no names]. Team climate: [description].
Propose a timed agenda with one well-described facilitation format, the instructions to read out, 8 open questions, a method to group the ideas and vote, and a way to turn the ideas into 3 actions at most, each with an owner and a date.
Add two tips so that everyone speaks up, including the quietest.

10. Write the project closeout report

To close properly and capture lessons.

The project [description] is finished. Data I have: initial objectives [list], results achieved [list], schedule and budget variances [description], difficulties encountered [list], positives [list].
Write a closeout report: 5-line summary, objectives met or not, variance analysis, lessons for future projects, recommendations, items to hand over to the maintenance or operations team.
Factual tone. Do not embellish any result: flag the claims for which I am missing evidence.

What to check

An AI proposes plausible breakdowns and durations, but it knows neither your team, nor your history, nor your hidden constraints. Compare every estimate with the opinion of the people who will do the work. A schedule generated without them is a hypothesis, not a commitment.

Data not to paste

Do not paste contracts, detailed budgets, colleagues' names or client data. Talk about "the technical team," "supplier A," "a budget of about [order of magnitude]." Check your company's policy on AI tools before handling project information with them.

Limits

The AI does not replace discussions with stakeholders: a useful risk register is built in a workshop, not alone in front of a screen. Use the answers as a checklist that keeps you from forgetting topics, then adapt to your method and your organization.

Frequently asked questions

Can AI make my schedule for me?

It can propose an outline and milestones, but durations depend on your team and your constraints. Have each estimate validated by the people doing the tasks, then adjust in your usual planning tool.

Can I paste a real report or budget into it?

It is better to avoid confidential documents and named data. Describe the situation in general or anonymized terms, and find out your company's rules on AI tools.

Do these prompts suit an agile method?

Yes, just state the method in the prompt (for example: two-week sprints, backlog, sprint review). Ask the AI to adapt the vocabulary and the deliverables to your way of working.
