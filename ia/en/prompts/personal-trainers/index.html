ChatGPT Prompts for Personal Trainers

Ten prompts to prepare your sessions, follow your clients and communicate faster, while staying within the limits of your profession.

  2. 8-week fitness program
  3. Initial client assessment
  4. Personalized motivation messages
  5. Plan social media content
  6. Exercise sheet with explanations for a client
  7. Handle a client who stopped or relapsed
  8. Description of your services and terms
  9. Mobility or stretching session
  10. Quarterly client review

A personal trainer designs workouts, follows clients, runs social media and manages a business. An AI helps you structure, vary your ideas and write your messages, based on what you know about each client.

Your role is athletic, not medical. The prompts below stay within that frame and invite you to refer clients to a health professional whenever a doubt appears. Always anonymize your clients' information.

  • Describe the client without a name or detailed health data.
  • Adapt every suggestion to the real person.
  • Refer to a doctor in case of medical doubt.

Replace the parts in [brackets] with your own details, then paste the prompt into ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Copilot or Le Chat. To adapt a prompt, use the prompt generator.

1. Build a group class

To prepare a varied session for a group.

You are a certified personal trainer. Build a group session of [length] minutes for [number] participants at [level] level, focused on [goal: cardio, strength, mobility]. Equipment available: [list]. Location: [gym, outdoors].
Format: table with warm-up, main part (blocks, duration, instruction, easier and harder variations) and cool-down. Add the safety instructions I must remind them of and the adaptations for a participant bothered by pain. State that any persistent pain calls for medical advice.

2. 8-week fitness program

To structure a progression for a client with no declared medical condition.

I am a trainer working with an adult client who is returning to exercise after a sedentary period. Their general profile: [approximate age, goals, availability of [number] sessions per week, equipment]. Do not take any medical condition into account: my client has a medical clearance provided and validated by their doctor.
Propose a progressive 8-week program with the progression principle, the typical content of a week and the criteria for moving to the next stage. Write a text I can hand to the client, stating that they must tell me about any pain and consult a health professional in case of an unusual symptom.

3. Initial client assessment

To run a structured first meeting.

I am preparing the first meeting with a new client. Create a 45-minute interview outline: questions about their goals, schedule, athletic experience, sleep and lifestyle, expectations, obstacles, preferences. Avoid medical diagnosis questions: include only one question asking whether they have their doctor's approval to exercise.
Finish with a 10-line report template that I fill in after the interview and with the list of points for which I must refer them to a doctor or another health professional.

4. Personalized motivation messages

To stay in touch between sessions.

Write five motivation messages of 40 words maximum for a client who [situation: lacks consistency, has reached a milestone, is going through a busy period]. Their goals: [goal]. Their style: [direct, gentle, humorous]. What they did well recently: [real fact].
Each message must encourage without guilt-tripping and suggest a simple 10-minute action. Forbidden: promising physical results, comparing with other people, remarks about weight or the body. The messages must sound like I wrote them.

5. Plan social media content

To run your account without spending the whole week on it.

You are a communications consultant for personal trainers. My business: [one-on-one coaching, group classes, online]. My audience: [description]. My tone: [tone]. Propose a 4-week calendar with 3 posts per week: practical tip, demonstration of a movement, testimonial approved by the client, behind the scenes.
For each post: hook, 50-word text, visual idea. No medical advice, no weight loss promises, no diets. Point out the content for which I must ask a client's written consent before publishing.

6. Exercise sheet with explanations for a client

To hand out a clear take-home guide.

Create an exercise sheet to give to a client for training at home: [number] exercises of [type: strength, mobility, balance], equipment [none, resistance band, light dumbbells]. For each exercise: goal, starting position, a 4-step description, number of repetitions to adapt, common mistake, easier variation.
Accessible language, no jargon. Add a safety box: stop in case of pain, abnormal shortness of breath or feeling unwell, and contact a health professional. I will review each instruction before handing it out.

7. Handle a client who stopped or relapsed

To re-engage a client who is dropping off without pushing them.

A client who trained with me has stopped for [length]. Known or assumed reason: [lack of time, motivation, reported injury, finances]. Write three messages: one to check in without pressure, one offering an adapted restart, and a last one leaving the door open.
Each message: 60 words, supportive tone, no guilt-tripping. If the reason is an injury or a health issue, the message must advise consulting a health professional and offer to resume only with their approval. Do not ask any medical questions.

8. Description of your services and terms

To clearly present your services on a website or in a document.

Write the "My coaching" page for my personal training business in [city or online]. My real services: [list with duration, content, audience]. My background and certifications: [exact list, without inventing anything]. My way of working: [description].
Structure: introduction, services, how a coaching program unfolds, frequently asked questions. Do not mention any guaranteed result or price. Include a sentence stating that coaching does not replace medical care. Leave [to be completed] for anything about contractual terms.

9. Mobility or stretching session

To enrich your programs with simple routines.

Propose a 15-minute mobility routine for clients who sit all day at work: [areas to work on: shoulders, hips, upper back]. Format: numbered exercises with duration, description of the movement, breathing, safety tip, variation.
The tone must stay athletic and preventive: no diagnosis, no promise of healing, no treatment of pain. Finish with a reminder: any persistent pain, numbness or significant limitation requires the advice of a doctor or a physical therapist.

10. Quarterly client review

To measure progress and set new goals.

I am preparing the three-month review with a client. Here are my anonymized notes: starting goals [list], sessions completed [number out of number planned], progress observed [facts: weights, endurance, consistency], difficulties [list], client feedback [quotes].
Write a one-page review with: what has improved, what still needs work, three goals for the next quarter, simple indicators, an encouraging conclusion. Add no measurement or health data that I have not provided, and never comment on weight or body composition as a diagnosis.

No medical advice

A personal trainer supports physical practice; they do not diagnose or treat. Do not use the AI to interpret a pain, a medical condition, a lab result, a treatment or a therapeutic diet. At the slightest doubt (pain, feeling unwell, medical history, pregnancy, chronic illness), refer your client to their doctor or another health professional and resume training with their approval.

Health data and privacy

Information about your clients' weight, health or injuries is sensitive data. Do not paste it into an AI tool with a name or anything that could identify them. Anonymize, stick to the essentials and respect the principles of privacy laws: a specific purpose, only the data that is necessary.

Promises and communication

Avoid any promise of physical results or weight loss in your posts and descriptions. Ask your clients for their consent before publishing a testimonial or a photo.

Limits

The AI offers generic sessions. It sees neither your client's posture nor their fatigue that day. Your observation takes priority over any generated program, which you must always adapt.

Frequently asked questions

Can AI replace my personalized training program?

No. It gives a base and ideas, but it does not see your client and cannot adapt a session live. Use it to structure and save time, then adjust with your experience and your observations.

Can I ask the AI for a meal plan for a client?

Avoid having it design an individual diet or nutrition plan. You can, however, ask it for general health education ideas, which you review, while referring to a health professional for particular cases.

What should I do if a client tells me about a pain?

Do not look for a diagnosis with the AI. Stop the exercise concerned, advise your client to see a doctor and resume only with their approval. Simply note the conversation in your tracking.
