Predict your race time

  • Calculated in your browser
  • Free, no limits
  • No sign-up
  • No data sent

From a recent result, estimate your time over 5K, 10K, half marathon and marathon, and see the limits of the prediction.

How to use it

  1. Enter the distance of your recent race or test, then your time in hours, minutes and seconds.
  2. Leave the exponent at 1.06 (original value); you can change it from 1.00 to 1.20.
  3. Read the table: predicted time, pace and speed over 5K, 10K, half marathon and marathon.
  4. Compare with your training: predicting a marathon from a 5K is very optimistic.

Formula: T2 = T1 × (D2 ÷ D1)1.06 (Riegel, 1981), where T1 is the time achieved over distance D1 and T2 the predicted time over D2. See Sources.

Where does this formula come from?

Peter Riegel, an American engineer and runner, observed that world records slow down steadily as the distance increases, between roughly 3.5 minutes and 230 minutes of effort.

He summed it up in the article "Athletic Records and Human Endurance" (American Scientist, 1981): multiply the time by the ratio of the distances raised to the power 1.06.

If the exponent were 1, the pace would be the same over every distance; at 1.06, doubling the distance multiplies the time by 2.09 instead of 2, a pace about 4% slower.

The limits, honestly

  • The formula is fitted on records and on runners trained for each distance: it predicts what you are worth if you train for the target distance.
  • Beyond the half marathon, it is often too optimistic for runners who do little mileage: a good 10K does not guarantee a marathon at the predicted time. Many users then raise the exponent (1.08 or 1.10), which lengthens the times.
  • Below 5K, it underestimates the effort linked to pure speed: do not use it for an 800 m.
  • The reference must be recent, run all out and on a flat course.

What to do with the result?

The predicted time helps you choose a goal pace, then build a plan: 10K plan, half marathon plan or marathon plan. The 10K, half marathon and marathon charts give the pace and the splits for each goal time.

Frequently asked questions

How do I predict my marathon time from a 10K?

With the Riegel formula, T2 = T1 × (42.195 ÷ 10)^1.06. A 10K in 50:00 gives about 3:50:01 for the marathon, but this prediction assumes a full, specific preparation.

Is the Riegel formula reliable?

It is a useful approximation between 5K and the half marathon for a trained runner. The further the predicted distance is from the reference, the less reliable it is, especially for the marathon.

Which exponent should I choose?

1.06 is Riegel's original value. If you run little mileage per week, a slightly higher exponent (1.08 to 1.10) gives more cautious predictions over long distances.

Which reference race is best?

A recent race (less than 6 to 8 weeks old), run all out on a flat course, preferably close to the target distance. A half-hearted training test is not suitable.

Are these results 100% accurate?

No: they are estimates based on published formulas. They give a good order of magnitude to organize your training, but your form on the day, the terrain, the weather and your experience can change the real result. Use them as a guide, not as a promise.
