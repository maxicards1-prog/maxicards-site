How to Run Longer Without Getting Out of Breath

The short answer

To run longer without getting out of breath, slow down until you can talk while running, then extend the time a little at a time, with walking breaks if needed. Breathlessness almost always comes from a pace that is too fast for your current fitness.

How to use it

  1. Slow down. Run at a pace where you can hold a conversation. If you can only say a few words, you are going too fast.
  2. Alternate running and walking. For example 1 minute of running, 1 minute of walking, repeated through the session. Walking lets your breathing settle without ending the workout.
  3. Lengthen the run, not the speed. From one week to the next, lengthen the running phases or the total time, but not both while also running faster.
  4. Run regularly. Two or three sessions a week work better than a single long, exhausting one.

Why do we get out of breath when running?

At the start, the most common cause is simple: going out too fast. The body is not yet used to the effort, and a pace that feels "normal" is actually too fast to hold for long. Slowing down is therefore the first lever, before any question of technique or breathing.

The talk test: a simple guide

The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) give an easy rule: at moderate intensity you can talk but not sing; at vigorous intensity you cannot say more than a few words without pausing for breath. To run longer, aim for the first case. It is the same idea as easy-run pace, explained with paces and heart rates.

Run-walk is a real method

Alternating running and walking is not failing: it is the simplest way to run longer while keeping your breathing under control. The guide run 30 minutes without stopping details a progression. For a first 5K (3.1 miles), see the beginner 5K plan.

Adjust your breathing

Controlled breathing helps, but it does not replace a suitable pace. Our guide on how to breathe while running explains rhythm and exhaling. If you get a side stitch, slow down and restart gently.

Track your progress

  • Note the total running time without walking, from one week to the next.
  • Work out your pace with the pace calculator to compare sessions of the same length (it shows min/km and min/mile).
  • If you wear a heart-rate watch, find your easy zone with our heart-rate zones.

The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends 150 to 300 minutes of moderate-intensity activity per week for adults, or 75 to 150 minutes of vigorous activity. Running a little but often is therefore a good starting point.

Sudden, unusual breathlessness, chest pain, palpitations or feeling faint are signs not to ignore: stop running and see a doctor. This article is general and does not replace medical advice.

Sources: CDC, measuring physical activity intensity (accessed 24/11/2026); WHO 2020 guidelines on physical activity (Bull et al., Br J Sports Med) (accessed 24/11/2026).

Frequently asked questions

How do I run for a long time without getting tired?

Run more slowly than you think you need to, alternate running and walking at first, and lengthen the time gradually. Fatigue mostly comes from a pace that is too fast.

How do I run without getting out of breath?

Pick a pace at which you can talk. If you can only say a few words, slow down or walk for a minute before restarting.

How can I run longer?

Increase your running time little by little, with two to three sessions a week, and keep a comfortable pace in every session.

How can I run faster and longer?

Both take time: build duration at an easy pace first, then add speed later (see the guide on how to run faster).
