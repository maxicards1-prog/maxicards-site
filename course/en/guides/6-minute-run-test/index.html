How to Do a 6-Minute Run Test Alone

The short answer

The 6-minute run test (half-Cooper) is done alone on a 400 m track: after a good warm-up, run as far as you can in 6 minutes at an even pace, then divide the distance in meters by 100 to get your MAS in km/h (or multiply miles by 10 for mph).

How to use it

  1. Choose a measured place. A 400 m track is ideal. Otherwise, a flat loop whose length you know (a GPS watch you have checked, or cones set every 100 m). Avoid slopes and strong wind.
  2. Warm up for 15 minutes. Very easy jog for 10 minutes, a few progressive accelerations of 80 to 100 m, then 3 minutes of walking or slow jogging. You should be warm, not tired.
  3. Start the timer. Start a 6-minute timer and set off at a hard but sustainable pace. The classic trap is starting too fast and falling apart in the 4th minute.
  4. Run evenly. Aim for a constant pace. Note your split at each lap if you can. Speed up only in the last minute if you have something left.
  5. Record the distance. At 6 minutes, stop, mark the spot on the ground and measure the total distance (number of laps x 400 m + the remainder).
  6. Calculate your MAS. Divide the distance in meters by 100. Example: 1,450 m (0.90 mile) gives 14.5 km/h (9.0 mph). Then walk for 5 to 10 minutes to recover.

What does the result mean?

The 6-minute test is a coaching convention: we assume that in 6 minutes a motivated runner covers roughly the distance run at their MAS.

Research (Billat et al., 2000) measured an average time to exhaustion of about 5 min 47 s at 100% of the speed associated with VO2 max, with great variability between runners. The test is therefore consistent, but it remains an estimate. We found no validity study dedicated to this test itself.

The 3.5 coefficient comes from Léger and Mercier (1984): it gives a good approximation of oxygen uptake between roughly 8 and 25 km/h. More details in VO2 max vs maximal aerobic speed.

Common mistakes

  • Starting too fast: an uneven pace costs you meters. The last laps should look like the first ones.
  • Measuring badly: a 50 m error changes the MAS by 0.5 km/h.
  • Testing while tired: avoid the day before a hard workout or a race.
  • Changing conditions: only compare tests done in the same place and in similar conditions.

What next?

The test is a maximal effort: if you are returning to exercise after a break, have pain, or have a heart or medical history, ask a doctor before doing it.

Frequently asked questions

Can you do the 6-minute test on a treadmill?

Yes, but results are rarely identical to the track. Set a 1% incline if you want to make up for the lack of wind, and then only compare with other treadmill tests.

Do I need to be fasted?

Avoid a large meal in the 2 to 3 hours before. A small light snack is fine; the point is not to have any digestive discomfort.

What if I do not have a track?

Use a flat straight line or a measured loop, or a GPS watch whose accuracy you have checked. Results will be less precise.

My result is very different from one test to the next. Why?

Form on the day, pacing, wind and warm-up all matter a lot. A variation of half a km/h (0.3 mph) is common.
