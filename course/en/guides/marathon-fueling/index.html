What to Eat and Drink During a Marathon

The short answer

Jeukendrup (2014) indicates that a single type of carbohydrate is absorbed up to about 60 g per hour, and that you can aim for close to 90 g/h with several types of carbohydrates beyond 2 h 30. For drinking, the 2015 consensus recommends following your thirst. Test everything in training.

Why eat during a marathon

The body's carbohydrate stores are limited. Beyond one to two hours of sustained effort, taking in carbohydrates during the race helps maintain pace. This article gives general guidelines; a doctor or sports dietitian can adapt them to your case (diabetes, digestive problems, medication).

How many carbs per hour?

Jeukendrup's review (2014, Sports Medicine) distinguishes two cases. For a long effort of 2 to 3 hours, the recommendation is about 60 grams per hour, from a single type of carbohydrate, because the gut cannot absorb much more.

For an effort of over 2 h 30 in trained athletes, you can aim for up to about 90 g per hour, provided you combine several carbohydrates absorbed through different pathways (for example glucose and fructose).

Many amateur marathoners run more than three hours: these guidelines therefore apply to them, but the gut can be trained and tolerance varies widely. Older ACSM recommendations spoke more of 30 to 60 g per hour.

In practice, carbohydrate content varies from one product to another: read the label rather than relying on a standard figure.

How much to drink?

On hydration, the 2015 consensus on exercise-associated hyponatremia (Hew-Butler et al., British Journal of Sports Medicine) recommends drinking according to thirst, which limits both excessive dehydration and the risk of drinking too much.

An older position spoke of individualizing intake based on sweat rate (Sawka et al., ACSM 2007); both approaches coexist, and heat changes the picture (see running in hot weather). Do not force yourself to drink when not thirsty: drinking too much is dangerous.

How to practice in training

  • Rehearse your fueling during long runs, at planned race pace.
  • Start with modest amounts and increase if your stomach accepts it.
  • Take small amounts at regular intervals rather than a lot at once.
  • Use the products offered on the course, or carry your own.

Planning with the pace chart

Your finishing time determines the length of the effort and so the number of intakes. Work it out with the race time calculator and the marathon pace chart, then note your fueling points.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Testing a product on race day.
  • Waiting until you are hungry or thirsty to start.
  • Drinking without thirst, on principle.

Significant nausea, dizziness, confusion or headaches during or after a race are signals not to ignore: ask for help on the spot.

Frequently asked questions

How many carbs per hour during a marathon?

Jeukendrup (2014) retains about 60 g/h for efforts of 2 to 3 h with a single carbohydrate, and up to about 90 g/h beyond 2 h 30 by combining several carbohydrates. Train your gut beforehand.

Should I drink at every aid station in a marathon?

The 2015 hyponatremia consensus recommends drinking according to thirst. Do not force yourself to drink if you are not thirsty.

What should I eat during a marathon?

Easily digested carbohydrates: gels, sports drinks, dried fruit or fruit chews. Choose what you have tested in training.

How many gels do I need for a marathon?

It depends on your finishing time and the carbohydrate content of each gel. Read the label, calculate grams per hour and test in training.
