How the Riegel Formula Predicts Race Times

The short answer

The Riegel formula predicts a time over one distance from a known time over another: T2 = T1 x (D2 / D1)^1.06. It is fairly reliable up to the half marathon, but often too optimistic for the marathon.

The formula

Peter Riegel, an American engineer, published in 1981 in American Scientist (vol. 69, pp. 285-290) a relationship between time and distance, built from records. It reads:

T2 = T1 x (D2 / D1)^1.06

T1 is your known time over distance D1; T2 is the predicted time over D2. The 1.06 exponent reflects the fact that we slow down a little as distance increases: time grows slightly more than distance.

How reliable is it?

The formula is only an average model. A study of recreational runners (BMC Sports Science, Medicine and Rehabilitation, 2016) found that it fits correctly up to the half marathon but clearly underestimates marathon times: runners are slower than predicted.

Informal analyses of runners point the same way and suggest higher exponents for long distances, but they are not published in scientific journals.

Why the gap at the marathon?

  • The marathon depends on training mileage and energy stores: a good 10K does not guarantee a good marathon.
  • Muscle fatigue and fueling weigh more over 3 hours than over 50 minutes.
  • Course profile, weather and level of preparation change everything.

How to use it wisely

  • Start from a recent, well-paced performance on a flat course.
  • To go from 10K to half marathon, the prediction is a good guide, provided you have done long runs.
  • For the marathon, aim for a slightly more cautious time than the prediction and read marathon pace.
  • Also use it to set goals for the pace calculator.

This formula replaces neither experience nor a coach's advice. Remember that insufficient preparation exposes you to injury: if you have any doubt about your health, see a doctor.

Frequently asked questions

What is the Riegel exponent?

1.06 in the original 1981 formula. Some studies use close values, such as 1.07, depending on the data.

Is Riegel reliable for a marathon?

Less than for shorter distances: a study of recreational runners shows it underestimates times. You need enough training volume.

Can I predict a 10K from a 5K?

Yes, with a good margin of error. Example: 25:00 for 5K gives about 52:07 for 10K.

Should I use a recent race?

Yes, to reflect your current level. Avoid an old time, an uphill race or one where you were injured.
