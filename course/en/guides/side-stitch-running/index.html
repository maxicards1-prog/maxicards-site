How to Get Rid of a Side Stitch

The short answer

A side stitch (exercise-related transient abdominal pain) has no proven cause. The most plausible hypothesis is irritation of the parietal peritoneum. In practice, slowing down, breathing deeply and changing your breathing rhythm often helps.

How to use it

  1. Slow down. Switch to walking or a very slow jog. Avoid stopping dead if you can.
  2. Breathe deeply. Inhale through the nose, exhale slowly through the mouth, expanding your belly.
  3. Press with your hand. Place your fingers on the painful area and press gently while you exhale.
  4. Change your breathing rhythm. Exhale when the opposite foot lands, for example. This shifts the stress on the organs.
  5. Resume gently. Pick the pace back up gradually as the pain eases.

What science knows

Researchers talk about "exercise-related transient abdominal pain" (ETAP). Morton and Callister (2015, Sports Medicine) reviewed the hypotheses: irritation of the parietal peritoneum (the membrane lining the abdomen), diaphragm ischemia, ligament strain, abdominal muscle cramps.

They conclude that parietal peritoneum irritation is the lead that best explains the observed characteristics, while stating that the question is not settled.

Commonly associated factors

Without a proven cause, several elements recur in the literature: eating or drinking just before running (notably very sugary drinks), intense effort at the start, a hunched posture, or breathing fatigue. These are leads, not guarantees. Each person has their own triggers.

How to prevent it

  • Avoid a big meal in the two to three hours before running, without making it a strict rule.
  • Warm up before raising the intensity: see the warm-up routine.
  • Keep your back straight and your shoulders relaxed.
  • Work on your breathing.
  • Start more slowly: the pace calculator helps you spot a pace that is too fast.

When to worry

A side stitch usually goes away when you slow down. See a doctor if the pain is intense, persists after you stop, comes with fever, vomiting, blood, unusual breathlessness, chest pain or pain radiating into one shoulder, or if it returns on every run. If in doubt, call a doctor.

This guide is general and does not replace medical advice.

Why many beginners get them

Starting too fast, shallow breathing and not warming up are common among beginners. The simplest solution remains alternating walking and running: see how to start running.

Frequently asked questions

Why do I get a side stitch when I run?

The exact cause is not demonstrated. The most studied hypothesis (Morton and Callister, 2015) is irritation of the parietal peritoneum. Starting too fast or eating too close to the effort are frequent triggers.

How do I get rid of a side stitch quickly?

Slow down, breathe deeply while exhaling slowly, gently press the painful area and change your breathing rhythm.

Is a side stitch dangerous?

Generally not; it goes away when you stop. But intense or persistent pain, with fever, vomiting or chest pain, calls for a doctor.

Should I eat before running to avoid a side stitch?

Mostly avoid a big meal right before. Some runners tolerate a light snack; test what suits you.
